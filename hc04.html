Set 1 · 9 min

Punctuation: six marks, six jobs

Apostrophe: omission or possession? Try to expand it. He's → he is ✓ letters missing = omission. Ronaldo's boots → "Ronaldo is boots" ✗ = possession. Careful: sister's in her room can expand (sister is). Names ending in s can take just an apostrophe: Carlos' shirt = possession.
Semi-colon joins two complete sentences that are closely linked. Test: could each side stand alone with a full stop? Madrid attacked; Barcelona defended.
Commas for extra information go around the part you could lift out: Arthur, who loved football, ran outside. Lift it out and the sentence still works.
Commas in a list separate the items. In British English there's usually no comma before the final and.
Inverted commas go around the spoken words only. If the speaker carries on after "cried the cyclist", that's a second pair of inverted commas.
Hyphen joins two words into one idea and changes the meaning: twenty-odd fans = about twenty fans. twenty odd fans = twenty strange fans.
Set 2 · 8 min

Sentences: change the shape

Passive: the thing that the action happens to moves to the front. Vinícius scored the goal. → The goal was scored by Vinícius. Keep the same tense.
Subject and verb must agree: one person → he plays. More than one → they play. Check who is doing the verb.
Command: starts with a bossy verb, ends with a full stop or exclamation mark, never a question mark. Pass me the ball, please.
Formal: no contractions (it'd, I'd), no chatty words, often passive. Your presence is requested… sounds like a letter from the King.
Simple past → simple present: played → plays · watched → watch · was → is · got → get. Check the subject first: my mum and I = two people.
Set 3 · 8 min

Word classes: what job is the word doing?

Verb or noun? The same word can be both. After a / the / his it's a noun: a walk. After a person doing it, it's a verb: we walk.
Determiners come before a noun and tell you which or whose: a, an, the, this, those, my, your, whose, which, some, every.
Prepositions show where or when: between, under, beside, behind, during, after.
Conjunctions join ideas: because (reason) · even though (surprise) · or (choice) · so (result).
Modal verbs show how sure: will / must = certain · might / could / may = possible.
Main clause makes sense on its own. Cover the part that starts with when / after / because…; what's left is the main clause.
Set 4 · 7 min

Word building: prefixes, suffixes, synonyms

Opposite prefixes: un- (unlucky) · im- before p and m (impatient, immature) · dis- (disagree) · in- (incorrect). Say it aloud: only one sounds like a real word.
Noun → adjective suffixes: -ful (hope → hopeful) · -ous (danger → dangerous) · -y (luck → lucky) · -ly (friend → friendly). Watch spelling: beauty → beautiful.
Synonym = same meaning (big → huge). Antonym = opposite (brave → cowardly). Keep the same word class: an adverb's antonym is an adverb (rapidly → slowly).

Set 5 · 8 min · notebook

Write it: seven quick ones

In your notebook. Number each one. Box Rule: neat, one line each, check before you tick.

  1. Passive: The goalkeeper saved the penalty.
  2. Command: Can you open the window?
  3. Present tense: Peggy kept the ball. She was careful. She got two letters.
  4. Add commas: Babaanne who had lived on Vine Street for forty years opened the envelope.
  5. Explain the difference: a dog-eating crocodile and a dog eating crocodile. One sentence for each.
  6. Synonym + antonym for: brave · tidy
  7. Noun → adjective: hope · danger · beauty
Answers: open only after you've written all seven
  1. The penalty was saved by the goalkeeper.
  2. Open the window, please. (bossy verb first, no question mark)
  3. Peggy keeps the ball. She is careful. She gets two letters.
  4. Babaanne, who had lived on Vine Street for forty years, opened the envelope.
  5. dog-eating = a crocodile that eats dogs · dog eating crocodile = a dog that is eating crocodile meat.
  6. brave: courageous / cowardly · tidy: neat / messy (other good answers count)
  7. hopeful · dangerous · beautiful
