The circulatory system

The heart is a pump. Nothing more, nothing less.

It is a muscle about the size of your fist, and it squeezes about 100,000 times a day. Every squeeze pushes blood round a loop:

Heart → lungs — blood picks up oxygen and drops off carbon dioxide
Lungs → heart → body — oxygen-rich blood goes out through arteries to muscles, brain, everything
Body → heart — used blood comes back through veins, and round it goes again
4chambers (2 atria on top, 2 ventricles below)
Arteriescarry blood AWAY from the heart (A for Away)
Veinscarry blood back IN to the heart
Bloodcarries oxygen, nutrients and water; takes away waste

Why does Ronaldo's heart beat slower?

Most people's hearts rest at about 60–100 beats per minute. Elite athletes are often around 40–50. Training makes the heart muscle bigger and stronger, so each squeeze pushes more blood — it doesn't need to beat as often. During a match it can go over 180 because the muscles are demanding oxygen fast.

🧪 Experiment · in your notebook

  1. Sit still 1 minute. Find your pulse (two fingers on the inside of your wrist, below the thumb). Count beats for 30 seconds, × 2. Write it: Resting: ___ bpm.
  2. Do 30 star jumps as fast as you can.
  3. Count again for 30 seconds, × 2. After: ___ bpm.
  4. Write one sentence: My heart beat faster because… (the word you need is oxygen).

Britain at war — the two wars

Arthur's dad fought in the first. Arthur is living through the second. Keep them apart in your head.

1914–1918The First World War (the Great War). Started by M.A.I.N. + the spark in July 1914.
1939–1945The Second World War. Britain declared war on Germany on 3 September 1939.
EvacueesChildren sent out of cities to the countryside to escape the bombing. Arthur thought Emre was one.
Anderson shelterA curved metal shed half-buried in the garden. Held about six people. Smelled of wet earth.

Coventry, 1940. The city made engines, aircraft parts and weapons, so it was a target. On the night of 14–15 November 1940, Coventry was bombed for hours; the medieval cathedral was destroyed and much of the city centre burned. Arthur's notebook is dated September 1940 — two months before that night.

Chapter 3 · The name on the cover

Emre did not tell Babaanne about Vine Street. He did not tell his mum, or his dad, or Kadir at school, who told everybody everything. He said he'd been "looking at old stuff" in the attic and had scared himself, which was true, and Babaanne made him drink a whole glass of milk and said that was what happened to boys who ignored rule number three.

But on Wednesday, after school, he went back.

The attic was exactly as he had left it. The crate. The blanket. The notebook lying open on the boards. He knelt beside it and, before he touched anything, he made himself look at the inside of the front cover.

There, in pencil, in Arthur's small slanting hand, was one word.

EMRE.

Emre sat back on his heels. The pencil looked old. The paper around it had gone yellow, and the letters had faded to grey, the way pencil does after eighty years. It had not been written on Saturday. It had been written in 1940, and it had been waiting.

He hesitated with his hand above the ball for a long time. Then he picked it up. It stayed warm, and ordinary, and nothing happened at all.

"Babaanne," he said at tea, as casually as he could manage, "who's Arthur?"

She did not stop stirring the pot. But she went still in a way Emre had never seen before, like a keeper who has already decided which way to dive.

"Where did you hear that name?"

"It's on a box. In the attic."

"Ah." She turned the heat down. "When your grandfather and I bought this house, in 1985, it belonged to an old lady. Mrs Bailey. Peggy. She had lived here her whole life." Babaanne wiped her hands slowly. "She left some things in the attic. She said she couldn't carry them and couldn't throw them away. I never looked. It felt… fragile. Like it wasn't mine to open."

"And Arthur was…?"

"Her brother." Babaanne finally looked at him. "She talked about him once. Only once. She said he had gone away in the war and never come back." She paused. "She didn't say he died, Emre. She said he vanished. I always thought that was a strange word to choose."

Emre put down his fork.

Upstairs, in a crate under a blanket, a leather ball that had just been perfectly warm began — very slowly — to go cold.

Q5 · In your notebook · 2 marks

How can you tell that Babaanne is hiding something when Emre says the name Arthur? Point → because → "quotation".

Point: Babaanne reacts as if the name is important and she doesn't want to show it. Evidence: "she went still in a way Emre had never seen before" and she answers his question with a question — "Where did you hear that name?"

Three new words

hesitate
verb

To pause before doing something because you're not sure.

The keeper hesitated, and the striker scored.

fragile
adjective

Easily broken or damaged; delicate.

The old notebook was fragile, so he turned the pages slowly.

vanished
verb

Disappeared suddenly and completely.

The ball vanished into the fog.
